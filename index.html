What’s happening:

The chemistry storyboard

Honey for glucose, violet for ethanol, blue for carbon dioxide, brown for yeast enzymes. The same colours run through every panel.

Panel 1: one sugar, four pieces

Yeast breaks each glucose molecule into two ethanol and two carbon dioxide molecules. Nothing is lost, only rearranged:

AtomsLeftRight
C62×2 + 2×1 = 6
H122×6 = 12
O62×1 + 2×2 = 6

Panel 2: the enzyme is the key

Yeast cells contain enzymes. An enzyme is a biological catalyst: glucose slots into its active site, the reaction happens much faster, and the enzyme is not used up. Without yeast, a glucose solution would sit there for years.

🧊Below 30 °C
enzymes work, but slowly
🫧30–40 °C
fast, steady fermentation
🔥Above 40 °C
enzyme changes shape and stops

Panel 3: Goldilocks temperature

Too cold and particles move slowly, so the rate drops (practical Q4). Too hot and the active site loses its shape, the enzyme is denatured, and fermentation stops (Q5). Your practical says 30–40 °C. Exam questions often quote about 25–35 °C. Either way, the idea is “warm, not hot”.

Panel 4: keep the air out

Industrial fermentation is done without air, because oxygen would oxidise the ethanol to ethanoic acid. Ethanol is also flammable. Burning it in plenty of oxygen:

C₂H₅OH + 3O₂ → 2CO₂ + 3H₂O

Panel 5: purifying it, the teacher demo

Fermentation stops at roughly 15% ethanol, because the yeast is poisoned by its own product. To get purer ethanol, the mixture is distilled. Ethanol boils at about 78 °C and water at 100 °C, so ethanol vapour comes over first, cools in the condenser and drips into the beaker. That is why you collect the 77–82 °C fraction. If it burns easily, it’s ethanol. Compared with making ethanol from ethene, fermentation uses a renewable source and mild conditions, but it is slower and gives an impure product.
